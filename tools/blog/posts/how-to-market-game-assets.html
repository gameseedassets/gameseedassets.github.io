Uploading an asset feels like launching it. It isn't. Unity says there are more than 12,000 active publishers on its store. On the day your package goes live, it sits in a "new" list for a little while, and after that the store mostly shows people the things that already have reviews, sales and good thumbnails.

So the question for a small publisher is not "how do I go viral". It's how to get the first few hundred of the right people onto your page, and how to make that page do its job when they land. This is what has worked for us, with our real numbers where we have them, and an honest "we don't know" where we don't.

Start with the store page, because everything points at it

Every other channel in this post sends people to a store page. If that page is weak, all of it leaks. Before you post anywhere, fix these:

  • The card image. At browse size it's 420×280 pixels in a grid of dozens. If you can't tell what the asset is from across the room, nobody clicks.
  • The first screenshot and the video. People open the gallery before they read a word.
  • The first two lines of the description. Say what it is and who it's for. "Complete Unity puzzle game template with a level editor" beats a paragraph about passion.
  • The compatibility table. Unity versions and render pipelines. A buyer on URP who can't see URP in that table just leaves.

We wrote a whole separate post on key images, screenshots and trailers because it's the highest-leverage thing on this list.

Free assets are the best marketing we've done

We have a handful of free packs on the Unity Asset Store: Fog Particles, Rain Particles, Low Poly Fire and the base Dark 2D World. They cost us real time to make and they earn nothing directly. Here's why we keep making them.

We counted the user ratings on each of our Unity listings on 9 November 2026. The free packs collect ratings far faster than anything paid:

Ratings are social proof, but the bigger effect is what a free pack does for your publisher page. Someone downloads Fog Particles, it works, and now they've seen your name, your docs and your support. The next time they need something, you're not a stranger.

The trick is to connect the free thing to the paid thing deliberately. Our free Dark 2D World and the paid Extended version use the same art, so anyone who likes the free one already knows what they'd get. The Extended description links straight back to the free version for anyone who wants to try the art first. It's a two-step funnel that lives entirely inside the store:

To be honest about the limits: we can't show you a clean "the free pack made X sales" number, because the store doesn't tell us where a buyer came from. What we can say is that the free packs are our most-rated listings, they're what people recognise us for, and they're the easiest things to share in communities without feeling like an ad.

If you try this, the free pack has to be good. A rough free asset doesn't just fail to help; it tells people what to expect from your paid ones.

One great quote beats a lot of noise

A while ago Code Monkey featured our Low Poly Fire Particles in one of his YouTube videos. He said one line about it:

"I love the cuteness of this one!"Code Monkey, on Low Poly Fire Particles

We put that line on our homepage and on the fire pack's page, with a link to the exact timestamp. A short, real quote from someone your audience already trusts does more than any amount of you describing yourself.

You can't arrange a feature like that, but you can make it easy to happen. Creators who review assets look for things that are visually obvious in a few seconds, free or cheap enough to try, and easy to drop into a scene. That describes good free VFX almost perfectly.

YouTube tutorials: slow, but they keep working

Several of our bigger assets have a tutorial video: how to set up the post-processing in Dark 2D World Extended, how to configure Paper Plane Controller. These aren't viral and they aren't meant to be. They do three jobs at once:

  • They answer the support question before someone has to ask it.
  • They show the asset working in a real editor, which a trailer can't.
  • They sit in YouTube search for years. People search "Limbo style game Unity" long after your launch week.

Put the link in the store description, in the documentation and on your own site. Unity also requires a setup video for tools and scripting packages, so if that's what you make, you're recording one anyway.

Support is marketing that happens to be useful

We offer every buyer three hours of free support, and we run a Discord server. It sounds like a cost. In practice it shows up in reviews. From our Stickman Shooter listing:

"Perfect template for me, I have built on top of this template and got a good project for my portfolio. Thanks a lot to the developer for quick support."
"Amazing asset for my web games website, also very quick support by the publisher!"

Two of the three reviews on that listing mention support. Nobody writes a review to say the package imported correctly. People write reviews when someone helped them.

Two practical notes. Reply to every store review, including the critical ones. And keep a single place to send people (for us that's Discord) so questions don't scatter across email, store comments and DMs.

Communities: Reddit, forums, Discord servers

Other developers are your customers, and they gather in a few predictable places: Reddit, the Unity forums, the Unreal forums for Fab assets, and genre-specific Discord servers. Each has its own rules about self-promotion, and getting those wrong gets you removed or quietly ignored.

This topic got big enough that we gave it its own post: Reddit for Unity Asset Store creators. The short version: post things that are interesting on their own (a free pack, a short loop, a breakdown of how something works) and let the link be secondary.

Your own website, even a small one

Store pages aren't yours. You can't change their layout, you can't add a page, and you can't see where visitors came from. A small site of your own fixes all three. Ours has a page for each of our main assets, with features, specs, the video, the FAQ and links to every store that sells it.

It also lets you show up in Google for searches the stores don't win, and lets Google connect your Unity, Fab, itch.io and YouTube accounts as one publisher. If you set one up, verify it in Google Search Console and submit a sitemap on day one. It's free and takes about half an hour.

Sales, bundles and timing

The Unity Asset Store runs regular promotions: Publisher of the Week, themed mega bundles, new release discounts. These are worth applying for and planning around, but they're Unity's decision, not yours.

What you do control is bundling your own work. We sell a 5 Games Bundle with five of our templates together. Bundles give an existing customer a reason to buy again, and they make every individual asset in them look like a better deal next to the bundle price.

On timing, the simplest rule we follow: don't announce anything until the store page is approved and you've looked at it in the preview. Launch posts that link to a "page not found" are a waste of a launch.

What we'd skip, or haven't tried

Buying followers or reviews. Obviously. It also violates store rules and it's easy to spot.

Posting the same link everywhere on launch day. Six subreddits in one afternoon reads as spam and usually gets treated as spam.

Paid ads. We haven't run paid ads for our assets, so we can't tell you whether they pay back. Given the 30% store cut and the size of a typical asset purchase, we'd want solid numbers on organic conversion before spending on them.

A weekly routine that fits around actual work

Marketing loses to development every time if it's a vague intention. A fixed, small routine works better:

WhenWhatTime
MondayReply to new store reviews and Discord questions20 min
WednesdayShare one thing: a loop, a breakdown, a free pack update, in one community30 min
FridayFix one thing on one store page: a screenshot, the first lines, the compatibility table30 min
MonthlyCheck which pages get views and ratings; update the weakest one1 hour

That's under two hours a week. It won't make an asset famous overnight. It does mean that every month there are a few more reviews, a few more people who've seen your name, and a slightly better page waiting for them when they arrive.
