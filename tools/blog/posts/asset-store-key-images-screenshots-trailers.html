Most buyers decide whether to click your asset from a picture 420 pixels wide, sitting in a grid with dozens of others. If they do click, they look at the gallery before they read a word, and if there's a video, they give it a few seconds. Everything else on the page is there to confirm a decision they've mostly already made.

That's why we spend more time on store media than feels reasonable. This post goes through the rules Unity enforces, and then takes apart our own media: the Grass Cutting Simulator cover and card, the captioned screenshots for Paper Plane Controller, and the trailer plans we work from.

The rules first, because a declined submission costs days

Unity asks for four key images at exact sizes. From its package details docs:

ImageSizeWhere it showsText allowed
Icon160 × 160Icon grid view, displayed at 80 × 80None
Card420 × 280The main browsing thumbnailTitle, publisher name or logo
Cover1950 × 1300Top of your product pageTitle, publisher name or logo, a tagline
Social media1200 × 630Unity's social posts and store homeNone

And for all of them: no Unity logos, no sale banners, no default Unity skybox in the background, nothing blurry, stretched or cropped, and not just screenshots of the Editor. Unity also recommends designing each size separately so the subject stays legible, instead of shrinking one big image four times.

That last recommendation is the one people skip, and it's the one that matters most.

The card: design for 420 pixels, not 1950

Here's what we mean. Below are the card and icon from our Grass Cutting Simulator media folder, shown at their real pixel size:

A few things we do on purpose here:

  • One subject, big. The blade and the cut grass fill the frame. No tiny characters, no busy UI.
  • The logo is chunky. At this size, thin fonts disappear. If you can't read it with your browser zoomed out to 50%, neither can a buyer.
  • The icon is redrawn, not resized. At 80 pixels on screen, the only thing that reads is a single shape and a strong colour.
One thing to fix before UnityLook at the line along the bottom of that card: "Complete game · 5 blades · 18 levels". It's useful, and fine on stores that allow it. Unity's rules only allow the title and publisher on a card, though, so for the Unity Asset Store version that line has to come off. The features move to the cover tagline and the screenshots instead. It's an easy thing to miss when you make one set of images for several stores.

The quickest test we know: put your card into a screenshot of the actual store grid, next to the ten best-selling assets in your category, and squint. If yours is the one your eye skips, change it before anything else.

The cover: the one place you get a tagline

The cover is big (1950 × 1300) and sits at the top of your product page, so it can carry more: the title, your publisher name, and one tagline. Ours for Grass Cutting Simulator is the hero image at the top of this post. The tagline is three short beats, "Mow it. Grow it. Own the field!", over the game's own art.

Use the tagline for the promise, not the feature list. "18 levels, 5 blades, boosts" is information; it belongs in screenshots and the description. The tagline is the reason someone wants it.

Screenshots: one idea each, with a caption

Unity calls screenshots "just as important as key images", and says curators prefer having them. We agree, with one addition: a raw screenshot shows what the asset looks like, a captioned one tells the buyer why it matters.

For Paper Plane Controller we keep two sets of every frame: clean screenshots with no text, and the same frames with captions in the pack's own HUD style. Here's the difference:

Rules we follow for captions:

  • Three to six words in the headline. "10 unique folds", "Flies in any world", "Ready-to-use HUD".
  • One feature per screenshot. If a frame needs two captions, it's two screenshots.
  • Show the feature working. The boost gauge is on screen while the caption talks about the HUD.
  • Be honest about what's not included. Our plane screenshots use third-party environments to look good, so they carry a small "environment art shown is not included" line. Buyers do read it, and it saves an angry review later.

For templates, include at least one screenshot of the tooling, not just the game. For Arrow Escape, the level editor is a reason to buy, so it gets its own captioned frame showing exactly what you do with it:

Trailers: 45 to 60 seconds, and the first three matter most

Our store trailers land around the 45 to 60 second mark. The Grass Cutting Simulator and Arrow Escape trailers both run about 47 seconds. That's long enough to show the whole product and short enough that people finish it.

Two things in that plan are worth copying even if the rest isn't:

Open on the product, moving. Your logo animation is the least interesting thing in the trailer, to everyone except you. The Grass Cutting trailer starts mid-level with the blade already spinning.

Separate "pretty" from "proof". Our scenic plane shots are staged on scripted paths to look good. The feature shots are the real controller, flown by an autopilot through its public input method, with the real HUD. We keep those two kinds of footage clearly apart, because buyers of a controller want proof that it actually flies like that.

Unity also has a specific requirement here: tools and scripting packages need a video tutorial showing setup and an overview of what the asset does, and animation packs need a video of every animation. A trailer is marketing; that tutorial is documentation. You'll often want both.

How we make all this without losing a week

Making media by hand for every asset and every store gets old fast. What helps us:

  • Capture inside Unity. We render store frames and trailer clips straight from the project with a small capture script, at full resolution, so every shot is repeatable. If the asset changes, we re-run the capture rather than re-staging everything.
  • Captions from a template. Captions are added by a script using the pack's own font and colours, so twenty screenshots stay consistent and a typo fix is one edit.
  • Keep a media folder per asset with the exact Unity sizes as filenames: "Card 420x280", "Cover 1950x1300", "Icon 160x160", "Social 1200x630". It sounds trivial, and it ends the "which version did we upload?" question.
  • Exclude the capture tooling from the package. It lives in its own folder that never ships.

A pre-upload checklist for store media

  • Icon 160×160 redrawn as a single shape, no text
  • Card 420×280 readable at 50% zoom, title and publisher only (on Unity)
  • Cover 1950×1300 with title, publisher and one tagline
  • Social 1200×630 with no text
  • No Unity logos, sale banners or default skybox anywhere
  • 6 to 10 screenshots, one captioned idea each, at least one showing the tooling
  • "Not included" note on any screenshot using third-party art
  • Trailer of 45 to 60 seconds that's moving in the first frame
  • Setup tutorial video if it's a tool, script or template
  • Same name and same key art on every store you sell on
